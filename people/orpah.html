Orpah (F)

First named in Ruth 1:4-14

OT Judges

Orpah was married to one of Naomi's sons during the family's years in Moab, alongside her sister-in-law Ruth, who married Naomi's other son (Ruth 1:4). After the deaths of Naomi's husband and both sons, Naomi resolved to return to Bethlehem and urged Orpah and Ruth to go back instead to their own mothers' homes and find new husbands among their own people, praying that the LORD would deal kindly with them as they had with the dead and with her (1:8-9). Orpah wept and initially resisted, insisting she would return with Naomi, but after Naomi pressed the point further -- pointing out she had no more sons to offer them and was too old to remarry and provide new ones -- "Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her" (1:10-14). Orpah's decision to return to Moab and "her gods" is recorded without moral condemnation; it was, by any ordinary measure, the more reasonable and secure choice, given Naomi's honest assessment that she had nothing left to offer her daughters-in-law (1:11-13, 15). Ruth's contrasting choice to stay -- "your people shall be my people, and your God, my God" -- is what the book goes on to celebrate, making Ruth an ancestor of David and ultimately of Christ (1:16-17; 4:13-22; Matthew 1:5). Orpah disappears from the narrative entirely after this scene, her story standing as the ordinary, reasonable road not taken beside Ruth's extraordinary loyalty.

Orpah was married to one of Naomi's sons while the family was living in Moab. After Orpah's husband, her brother-in-law, and her father-in-law all died, Naomi decided to return to her homeland of Bethlehem and encouraged both of her Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, to go back to their own families and start new lives instead of following her. Orpah loved Naomi and wept at the thought of leaving her, but after much urging, she eventually kissed Naomi goodbye and returned home to her own people. Ruth, on the other hand, chose differently, famously refusing to leave Naomi's side. Orpah's decision wasn't shown as wicked in the Bible -- just a real, understandable choice -- but her story is remembered mostly as the different path not taken, standing beside Ruth's much more famous choice of loyalty and faith.
