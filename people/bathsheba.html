Bathsheba (F)

Also called: Bathshua
Name means “The seventh daughter; the daughter of satiety” — Hebrew

First named in 2 Samuel 11

OT United Monarchy

While her husband Uriah, one of David's elite warriors, was at war, David saw Bathsheba bathing, sent for her, and she came; when she conceived, David recalled Uriah to sleep with his wife to cover it up, and when Uriah refused out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, David had him killed in battle (2 Samuel 11:2-21). Scripture assigns the moral verdict plainly and only to David: 'the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of the LORD' (2 Samuel 11:27). On learning of Uriah's death, Bathsheba mourned him, and only afterward was she brought into David's house as a wife (2 Samuel 11:26-27). The prophet Nathan confronted David with a parable in which David alone is condemned; God's judgment included the death of the child born from the affair (2 Samuel 12:1-19). Their second son was Solomon, given the name Jedidiah, 'beloved of the LORD,' by Nathan — grace following judgment (2 Samuel 12:24-25). Decades later, with David old and Adonijah moving to seize the throne, Bathsheba acts decisively: at Nathan's urging, she reminds David of his oath that Solomon would reign, securing his public confirmation before he died (1 Kings 1:11-31). After Solomon's coronation, Bathsheba appears again as queen mother, carrying a request from Adonijah that Solomon reads as a renewed threat and answers by executing him (1 Kings 2:13-25). Matthew's genealogy of Jesus names her, pointedly, as 'the wife of Uriah' (Matthew 1:6) — grace, not respectability, shaping the Messiah's ancestry.

Bathsheba went through something very painful — King David saw her, and made choices that hurt her deeply and led to the death of her husband, a loyal soldier named Uriah. Even though what happened wasn't Bathsheba's fault, it changed the course of her life. Later, God still blessed her: her son Solomon became Israel's next great king, and Bathsheba herself helped make sure Solomon actually received the throne when others tried to take it first. Bathsheba is remembered in the Bible as an important part of the family line that eventually led to Jesus. Bathsheba's story shows that God can bring good purposes out of even the most painful situations in our lives.

Thought for Today

Bathsheba’s story teaches us about consequence and grace; may we look to Jesus, whose grace and truth never fail.

References: 2 Samuel 11; 2 Samuel 12:1-25; 1 Kings 1; 1 Kings 2:13-25; Matthew 1:6
