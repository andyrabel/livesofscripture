Bathsheba

Also called: Bathshua
OT United Monarchy

⚠️ AI-generated — not yet human reviewed

Wife of Uriah the Hittite, summoned by King David while her husband was at war; after David arranged Uriah's death to cover the resulting pregnancy, she became David's wife and, decades later, successfully secured the throne for her son Solomon against a rival claimant.

For younger readers

Bathsheba went through a very hard time, but later became Solomon's mother and helped him become king.

Interpretive note: 2 Samuel 11 gives Bathsheba almost no voice or interiority in the initial encounter with David — 'he sent messengers, and took her.' Interpreters differ over how much agency the text implies she had in a situation where a reigning king summoned a subject's wife: some read the episode as mutual sin, others emphasize the severe power imbalance and read David's action as closer to abuse of royal authority than to a two-sided affair, noting that Nathan's parable names David alone as the guilty 'rich man' (2 Samuel 12:1-6) and that 2 Samuel 11:27 states plainly that 'the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of the LORD' without naming Bathsheba's culpability. Scripture does not state her intent, and this site does not pick a side.

Life Story

While her husband Uriah the Hittite, one of David's own elite warriors, was away at war, David saw Bathsheba bathing from his palace roof, sent messengers for her, and she came; when she conceived, David tried to cover it up by recalling Uriah from the front to sleep with his wife, and when Uriah refused out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers still in the field, David had him killed in battle by deliberately exposing him to the enemy (2 Samuel 11:2-21). Scripture assigns the moral verdict for this plainly and only to David: 'the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of the LORD' (2 Samuel 11:27). On learning of Uriah's death, Bathsheba mourned him, and only afterward was she brought into David's house as a wife (2 Samuel 11:26-27). The prophet Nathan confronted David with a parable of a rich man stealing a poor man's only lamb, in which David alone is condemned; God's judgment included the death of the child born from the affair, a loss Bathsheba's own grief over is implied though not narrated in detail (2 Samuel 12:1-19). David and Bathsheba's second son together was Solomon, given the name Jedidiah, 'beloved of the LORD,' by Nathan himself — a sign of grace following judgment (2 Samuel 12:24-25). Decades later, with David old and his son Adonijah moving to seize the throne before his father's death, Bathsheba appears as a decisive political actor: at the prophet Nathan's urging, she goes to David and reminds him of his earlier oath that Solomon would reign, securing David's public confirmation of Solomon before he died (1 Kings 1:11-31). After Solomon's coronation, Bathsheba appears once more as queen mother, carrying — and then declining to fully back — a request from Adonijah that Solomon reads as a renewed threat to his throne and answers by having Adonijah executed (1 Kings 2:13-25). Matthew's genealogy of Jesus names her, pointedly, not by her own name but as 'the wife of Uriah' (Matthew 1:6), in a genealogy that also names Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth — each a woman touched by scandal or foreignness, underscoring grace rather than respectability as the shape of the Messiah's ancestry.

Family

Bathsheba's father is named as Eliam (2 Samuel 11:3), possibly the same Eliam identified elsewhere as the son of David's counselor Ahithophel (2 Samuel 23:34) — a connection some interpreters draw and others resist, but one that, if correct, would make Ahithophel's later betrayal of David during Absalom's rebellion (2 Samuel 15-17) a strand of the same family's grievance against the king. Bathsheba was first married to Uriah the Hittite, then to David after Uriah's death; her first son with David died in infancy, and her second son, Solomon, succeeded David as king through her own direct intervention at the end of David's life (1 Kings 1).

References: 2 Samuel 11; 2 Samuel 12:1-25; 1 Kings 1; 1 Kings 2:13-25; Matthew 1:6
