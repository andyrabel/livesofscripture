Gallio (M)

Name means “Who sucks” — Latin

First named in Acts 18:12-17

NT Apostolic

Gallio, the Roman proconsul of the province of Achaia, presided at Corinth when Jewish leaders united to bring Paul before his judgment seat, accusing him of "persuading men to worship God contrary to the law" (Acts 18:12-13). Before Paul could even offer a defense, Gallio cut the proceedings short, ruling that since the charge involved "words and names and your own law," it fell outside Roman jurisdiction: "I am unwilling to be a judge of these matters... I do not wish to be a judge of these things" (18:14-15). He drove the accusers away from the judgment seat (18:16). Immediately afterward, the crowd seized Sosthenes, a synagogue official, and beat him in front of the tribunal, "but Gallio was not concerned about any of these things" (18:17). Gallio's studied indifference to the whole dispute was not sympathy for Paul's message -- he shows no interest in the theological content at all -- but his ruling had the practical effect of establishing, in a major Roman province, that Christian preaching was not a punishable civil offense, a precedent that likely protected early Christian missionaries from similar legal harassment elsewhere in the empire. Gallio is identified in Roman records as the brother of the philosopher Seneca, and an inscription discovered at Delphi dating his proconsulship has become a key fixed point for dating Paul's ministry in Corinth.

Gallio was the Roman governor of the province where Corinth was located. When some people who opposed Paul dragged him before Gallio's judgment seat, accusing him of teaching against the law, Gallio refused to even hear the case. He told them this was a dispute about Jewish religious teaching, not a real crime under Roman law, and he sent them away. When the crowd then beat another man named Sosthenes right in front of him, Gallio didn't concern himself with that either. Though Gallio wasn't trying to help Paul specifically, his refusal to get involved ended up protecting Paul from a serious legal threat.

Thought for Today

Gallio's ruling that Christian preaching broke no Roman law protected early believers, though he had no interest in Christ. God can use indifferent rulers to protect Jesus' gospel.
