Tiglath-pileser (M)

Also called: Tilgath-pilneser

First named in 2 Kings 15:29

OT Divided Monarchy

Tiglath-pileser III (called Tilgath-pilneser in Chronicles) was the powerful king of Assyria whose westward campaigns reshaped the politics of Israel and Judah in the eighth century BC. He invaded northern Israel during the reign of Pekah, capturing Gilead, Galilee, and the land of Naphtali and carrying their inhabitants into exile in Assyria -- the beginning of the deportations that would culminate in the northern kingdom's fall (2 Kings 15:29; 1 Chronicles 5:26). When Judah's king Ahaz faced an alliance of Israel and Aram-Damascus against him, he stripped silver and gold from the temple and his own palace and sent it to Tiglath-pileser as a bribe, calling himself Assyria's "servant and son" and begging for rescue (2 Kings 16:7-8). Tiglath-pileser obliged, marching against Damascus, capturing it, and killing King Rezin -- but Chronicles records the deal soured Judah rather than saved it: "he came against him and afflicted him instead of strengthening him," since Ahaz's tribute only bought temporary relief at the cost of Judah's independence and treasury (2 Kings 16:9; 2 Chronicles 28:20-21). Ahaz later traveled to Damascus to meet his new overlord and, struck by an altar he saw there, had a copy built and installed in the Jerusalem temple in place of the LORD's own altar (2 Kings 16:10-16). Isaiah had already warned Ahaz that trusting Assyria instead of the LORD would bring "the king of Assyria" as an instrument of judgment on Judah itself, a warning this alliance fulfilled generations later (Isaiah 7:17).

Tiglath-pileser was a powerful king of Assyria, a strong empire to the east of Israel and Judah. He attacked northern Israel and took many people away from their homes to Assyria -- the start of hard times for God's people there. Later, when Judah's king Ahaz felt threatened by his neighbors, Ahaz gave Tiglath-pileser gold and silver from God's temple, asking him for help instead of trusting God. Tiglath-pileser did help defeat Ahaz's enemies, but the Bible says this didn't really make Judah stronger -- it actually caused Judah more trouble in the end. Ahaz even copied an altar he saw in Assyria and had one built in God's temple instead of following God's own instructions. Tiglath-pileser's story is a warning about what happens when people trust powerful nations instead of trusting God.

Thought for Today

Ahaz trusted Tiglath-pileser's army instead of God, and it cost him everything. May we run to God first, not to whatever looks strong.

References: 2 Kings 15:29; 2 Kings 16:7-18; 1 Chronicles 5:6-26; 2 Chronicles 28:20-21; Isaiah 7:17
