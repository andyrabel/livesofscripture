Abishag (F)

Name means “Ignorance of the father” — Hebrew

First named in 1 Kings 1:3-15

OT United Monarchy

As King David grew old and could not keep warm, his servants searched all Israel for a beautiful young woman to attend him, and found Abishag the Shunammite. She became his attendant and nurse, but Scripture states plainly that David "did not know her" intimately (1 Kings 1:1-4). She next appears after David's death, when Adonijah -- the son who had earlier tried to seize the throne before Solomon was confirmed as king -- came to Bathsheba and asked her to request Abishag as his wife from Solomon (2:13-18). Bathsheba brought the request to Solomon, who reacted with alarm rather than indifference: asking for a former royal attendant carried the weight of a political claim to the throne itself, since taking a king's household could symbolize succession. Solomon responded, "Why do you ask Abishag the Shunammite for Adonijah? Ask for him also the kingdom!" and had Adonijah executed that same day (2:22-25). Abishag herself never speaks or acts in either account; she is entirely passive, first as a caregiver assigned to warm an old king's failing body, then as an unwitting pawn in a deadly succession dispute she had no part in starting. Her story illustrates how vulnerable a woman with no power of her own could be, caught between the ambitions of powerful men in David's court.

Abishag was a young woman from the town of Shunem who was brought to the royal court to take care of King David when he was very old and frail. She served him faithfully in his final days. After David died, David's son Adonijah -- who had once tried to take the throne before Solomon -- asked to marry Abishag. Solomon, now king, saw this request as a dangerous political move rather than an innocent one, and it led to serious consequences for Adonijah. Abishag herself is never shown speaking or acting on her own in the story; she becomes, without choosing it, part of the tense struggle over who would truly rule after David.

Thought for Today

Abishag had no voice in the palace intrigue that decided her fate. Jesus lifts up the powerless and gives dignity to those the world overlooks.

References: 1 Kings 1:3-15; 1 Kings 2:17-22
