Elihu

Also called: Eliab, Eliel
OT Patriarchal

A younger man who spoke last among Job's counselors, angry both at Job for justifying himself and at the three friends for failing to answer him, arguing that suffering can serve to discipline and instruct rather than only punish.

For younger readers

Elihu was a younger man who had been listening quietly to the whole conversation between Job and his three older friends. He was frustrated with all of them -- with the friends for not being able to really answer Job's questions, and with Job for seeming to defend his own innocence more than he defended God. Once everyone else had finished speaking, Elihu spoke up, offering a somewhat different perspective: that suffering isn't always a direct punishment, but can sometimes be a way God gets a person's attention, teaches them, or humbles them. His long speech about God's greatness and power in nature helps set the stage right before God Himself finally speaks to Job out of a whirlwind.

Interpretive note: Unlike Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, Elihu is never named among the friends God rebukes at the book's close (Job 42:7-9), and his speeches (chapters 32-37) introduce themes -- that suffering can refine and instruct rather than only punish -- that anticipate God's own speeches from the whirlwind. Interpreters are divided on how to read this: some see Elihu as a genuinely wiser, closer-to-correct voice bridging the friends' failed arguments and God's answer; others read his lengthy self-important introduction (32:6-22) and his claims to have understanding rivaling the friends' as itself a form of the same overconfident presumption the book critiques, since God's speeches that follow immediately overwhelm and correct him along with everyone else. Scripture leaves his exact status genuinely ambiguous, and this site does not take a side.

Life Story

Elihu, a younger man, had listened silently throughout the entire dialogue between Job and his three friends out of deference to their age, but grew angry once they finished speaking -- angry at Job for justifying himself rather than God, and at the three friends for failing to answer Job convincingly despite condemning him (Job 32:1-5). He introduces himself with a lengthy claim that "years should speak" but that "it is a spirit in a man, and the breath of the Almighty gives him understanding," positioning his youth as no obstacle to genuine wisdom (32:6-9). Across four speeches (chapters 32-37), Elihu offers a perspective distinct from the three friends: rather than insisting Job's suffering must prove hidden sin, he argues God sometimes uses pain, dreams, and affliction to warn a person away from pride, to discipline and instruct rather than merely punish, opening a person's ear "in time of their affliction" (33:14-30). He also rebukes Job for words that came close to charging God with injustice, while extensively praising God's incomprehensible greatness in the natural world -- the rain, lightning, and clouds -- as a warm-up to the whirlwind speech that follows immediately from God Himself (36:26-37:24). Notably, Elihu alone among Job's counselors is never named or rebuked in God's closing words to the three friends (42:7), leaving his exact standing in the book's argument a matter Scripture does not spell out (see interpretive note).

First named in 1 Samuel 1:1
