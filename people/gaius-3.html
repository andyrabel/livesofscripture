Gaius

NT Apostolic

The 'beloved' believer to whom John addressed his third letter, commended for his faithfulness and hospitality in supporting traveling missionaries and strangers, in implicit contrast to the unwelcoming Diotrephes described later in the same letter.

For younger readers

Gaius was a believer whom the apostle John cared for deeply -- John's third letter in the Bible is written directly to him, and John calls him 'beloved' four different times in just a short letter. John praised Gaius for his faithfulness and especially for his hospitality, since Gaius had been generously supporting traveling missionaries and strangers who came through, providing for their needs even though they were people he'd never met before. This stood in sharp contrast to another man mentioned later in the same letter, Diotrephes, who refused to welcome traveling believers at all. Gaius's warm, generous hospitality toward God's traveling servants made him a model example John was glad to hold up and encourage.

Interpretive note: This Gaius, addressed directly as the recipient of John's third letter, is a common name shared by several other New Testament figures (Paul's host in Corinth of Romans 16:23 and 1 Corinthians 1:14; his Macedonian traveling companion in Acts 19:29; Gaius of Derbe in Acts 20:4); the different geographic and chronological contexts lead most interpreters to treat this Gaius, associated with John's circle of churches likely in Asia Minor, as a distinct individual from the others, though the common name means certainty is not possible.

Life Story

Gaius is the direct recipient of John's third letter, addressed simply as 'the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth' (3 John 1:1), a term of affection John repeats four times across this short letter. John opens by praying for Gaius's health and prosperity to match the evident well-being of his soul, and rejoices greatly at hearing from visiting believers that Gaius was 'walking in truth' (1:2-4). John's central commendation concerns Gaius's hospitality: he had been faithful in whatever he did for the traveling brothers, even though they were strangers to him personally, providing for their needs 'in a manner worthy of God' as they went out for the sake of Christ's name, accepting nothing from unbelievers (1:5-7). John urges Gaius to continue supporting such workers, 'so that we may be fellow workers with the truth' (1:8) -- framing hospitality toward traveling ministers as genuine partnership in the spread of the gospel itself, not merely an act of kindness. This commendation is placed in deliberate contrast with Diotrephes, described just afterward as a man who loved to be first, refused to welcome the same traveling brothers, and even expelled from the church anyone who tried to (1:9-10). Gaius's consistent, welcoming faithfulness toward strangers serving Christ stands as the clear positive model John holds up for the whole letter, alongside his closing commendation of Demetrius.
