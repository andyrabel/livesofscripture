Ruth

OT Judges

A Moabite widow who bound herself to her mother-in-law Naomi and to Naomi's God, gleaned in the fields of Boaz, and became through him the great-grandmother of King David — an outsider grafted directly into Israel's covenant line and named in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus.

For younger readers

Ruth was a woman from Moab who lost her husband, but instead of leaving her mother-in-law Naomi to go back to her own family, Ruth chose to stay loyally by Naomi's side and follow Naomi's God. This was a really big decision, since Ruth was leaving her whole home behind for an uncertain future. In Bethlehem, Ruth worked hard gathering leftover grain to take care of herself and Naomi, and a kind relative named Boaz noticed her faithfulness and married her. Ruth became the great-grandmother of King David, and she's remembered as an example of true loyalty and faithfulness, even when it meant giving up everything familiar. Ruth's story shows that God welcomes and blesses faithful people, no matter where they come from.

Life Story

Ruth was a Moabite widow, married into an Israelite family that had settled in Moab during a famine, who lost her husband Mahlon along with her father-in-law and brother-in-law, leaving her mother-in-law Naomi without support (Ruth 1:1-5). When Naomi decided to return to Bethlehem and urged her daughters-in-law to stay behind and remarry among their own people, Ruth refused, binding herself to Naomi, Naomi's people, and Naomi's God in one of Scripture's most quoted statements of loyalty (Ruth 1:16-17). This was no small thing — Moabites were barred by the law of Moses from Israel's assembly for ten generations (Deuteronomy 23:3-6) — and Ruth's story is partly about God's grace overriding that exclusion (Ruth 2:12). In Bethlehem, Ruth supported herself and Naomi by gleaning grain, coming under the notice of Boaz, a relative of Naomi's late husband, whose kindness went beyond what the law required (Ruth 2). Naomi recognized Boaz as a potential kinsman-redeemer and guided Ruth through the culturally coded process of asking him to fulfill that role, which he did after a nearer relative declined (Ruth 3-4). Ruth is never shown converting in an evangelistic sense; the text presents her covenant loyalty as already settled fact, treating her faith the way it treats Abraham's — trust credited without a conversion narrative. Her marriage to Boaz produced Obed, grandfather to Jesse and great-grandfather to David — a Moabite woman becoming, by God's providence, the great-grandmother of Israel's greatest king (Ruth 4:13-22; Matthew 1:5).
