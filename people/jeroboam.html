Jeroboam

OT Divided Monarchy

First king of the northern kingdom of Israel after the split from Judah, whose golden calves at Bethel and Dan became the paradigm sin — "the sin of Jeroboam" — by which every later northern king is measured and condemned.

For younger readers

Jeroboam became the first king of the ten northern tribes after Israel split into two kingdoms. God had promised to bless Jeroboam's family if he obeyed, but Jeroboam was afraid his people would want to go back to the other king if they kept traveling to Jerusalem to worship. So instead of trusting God's promise, Jeroboam made two golden calf statues and told people to worship there instead — which broke God's clear command not to worship idols. This started a pattern of idol worship that hurt Israel for generations afterward. Even when God showed Jeroboam a warning miracle, he didn't change his ways. Jeroboam's story teaches that fear can tempt even a leader to disobey God, and that one bad choice can affect an entire nation for a very long time.

Life Story

Jeroboam was an official under Solomon whom the prophet Ahijah met on a road and told, by tearing a new garment into twelve pieces, that God was giving him ten tribes as judgment on Solomon's idolatry — with a conditional promise of an enduring dynasty if Jeroboam kept God's statutes like David (1 Kings 11:26-38). Solomon tried to kill him, and Jeroboam fled to Egypt, returning to become king over the ten northern tribes when Rehoboam's harsh answer split the kingdom (1 Kings 11:40; 12:1-20). Rather than trust the promise, Jeroboam feared his people would return to Rehoboam if they kept traveling to Jerusalem to worship, so he made two golden calves at Bethel and Dan and told Israel, "Behold your gods... who brought you up from Egypt," installing his own priests against the first and second commandments (1 Kings 12:26-33). A man of God confronted him at Bethel's altar; it split, and Jeroboam's hand withered when he ordered the prophet seized, restored only after the prophet's prayer — yet Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way (1 Kings 13:1-34). This became Scripture's standing indictment of the whole northern kingdom: king after king is measured by whether he "walked in the sin of Jeroboam," and 2 Kings 17:21-22 ties Israel's exile directly to this legacy. Ahijah later told Jeroboam's wife their sick son alone would be mourned, and the rest of his house swept away — fulfilled when Baasha assassinated Jeroboam's son and successor Nadab (1 Kings 14:1-18; 15:27-29).
