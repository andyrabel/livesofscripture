Samuel

OT Judges

⚠️ AI-generated — not yet human reviewed

Born to Hannah in answer to prayer and dedicated to the LORD from infancy, Samuel served as Israel's last judge and a prophet who anointed both Saul and David as king, confronting Saul's disobedience directly and mourning him even after rejecting him as king.

For younger readers

Samuel was given to God by his mother Hannah's prayer, and grew up to be a prophet who anointed Israel's first two kings.

Interpretive note: 1 Samuel 28:3-19 describes a figure identified as Samuel appearing after Saul consults a medium at En-dor, despite Samuel having forbidden such practices during his lifetime. Interpreters are divided over whether this was genuinely Samuel's spirit, permitted by God for this one final pronouncement of judgment on Saul, or a deceptive apparition. Scripture does not explain the mechanism, only that a true and devastating prophecy of Saul's coming death was delivered.

Life Story

Samuel was born to Hannah, a barren woman who prayed with such visible anguish at Shiloh that the priest Eli mistook her for drunk, and who vowed that any son God gave her would be given back to serve the LORD for life (1 Samuel 1:1-20). True to her vow, once Samuel was weaned Hannah brought him to Shiloh to live and serve under Eli, visiting him only once a year with a handmade robe (1 Samuel 1:24-28; 1 Samuel 2:19). As a boy still serving in the tabernacle, Samuel heard the LORD calling his name in the night and, after mistaking the voice for Eli three times, was instructed to answer, 'Speak, LORD, for Your servant is listening' — and the first prophetic message he received as a child was a hard one, a judgment against Eli's own household for the corruption of Eli's sons, which Samuel faithfully delivered the next morning despite the difficulty (1 Samuel 3:1-18). He grew to be recognized throughout Israel as a prophet whose word never failed (1 Samuel 3:19-20). As an adult, Samuel led Israel in repentance and a decisive victory over the Philistines at Mizpah, setting up a memorial stone called Ebenezer, 'thus far the LORD has helped us,' and judged Israel for the rest of his life on a circuit between Bethel, Gilgal, Mizpah, and his home in Ramah (1 Samuel 7:3-17). In his old age, with his own sons taking bribes and perverting justice as judges, the elders of Israel demanded a king 'like all the nations' — a request Samuel took personally, but which God clarified was really a rejection of God's own kingship over them; Samuel warned the people plainly what a king would cost them in taxes, labor, and sons, then anointed Saul at God's direction anyway (1 Samuel 8; 1 Samuel 10:1). Samuel confronted Saul's disobedience twice: first for offering a sacrifice only Samuel was authorized to make, and decisively when Saul spared King Agag and the best Amalekite plunder against God's explicit command, prompting Samuel's famous rebuke that 'to obey is better than sacrifice' and the announcement that God had rejected Saul as king (1 Samuel 13:8-14; 1 Samuel 15:10-29). Even so, Samuel grieved over Saul until God told him to stop mourning and go anoint his replacement (1 Samuel 15:35-16:1). At Bethlehem, Samuel anointed the youngest and least likely of Jesse's eight sons, David, after God told him not to judge by outward appearance, 'for the LORD looks at the heart' (1 Samuel 16:1-13). Samuel died, and all Israel mourned him (1 Samuel 25:1), but he appears once more after death: when a desperate Saul consults a medium at En-dor, a figure identified as Samuel pronounces Saul's coming death and Israel's defeat — an episode Scripture reports without fully explaining how it happened (1 Samuel 28:3-19).

Family

Samuel was the son of Elkanah and Hannah, born in direct answer to Hannah's prayer and vow, and raised from early childhood at Shiloh under the priest Eli rather than in his parents' home, though Hannah visited yearly (1 Samuel 1-2). In his own old age, Samuel's sons Joel and Abijah, whom he had appointed as judges over Israel, took bribes and perverted justice, becoming part of Israel's stated reason for demanding a king (1 Samuel 8:1-5) — a generational echo of the very corruption in Eli's sons that Samuel himself had once delivered God's judgment against as a child. Samuel stands at the hinge of Israel's history, anointing both of its first two kings and closing out the era of the judges.

References: 1 Samuel 1-3; 1 Samuel 7-12; 1 Samuel 15-16; 1 Samuel 28:3-19
