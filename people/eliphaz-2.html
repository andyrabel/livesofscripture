Eliphaz (M)

First named in Job 2:11

OT Patriarchal

Eliphaz the Temanite was the first and, judging by his position speaking first in each round of dialogue, likely the eldest of Job's three friends, who traveled together to comfort Job after his catastrophic losses, sitting with him in silent grief for seven days before anyone spoke (Job 2:11-13). Once the dialogue began, Eliphaz argued from a rigid retributive framework: "who, being innocent, has ever perished? Or where were the upright destroyed?" -- reasoning that since God is just, suffering of this magnitude must be punishment for hidden sin (4:7-8). He appealed to a mysterious night vision as the source of some of his claims about human unrighteousness before God (4:12-21), and his tone hardened across his three speeches, eventually accusing Job outright of specific sins -- oppressing widows and orphans -- that the narrative gives no basis for believing Job actually committed (22:5-9). Eliphaz's theology was not wrong in principle (sin does have consequences), but wrong in its rigid application to Job's case, since the reader already knows from the book's opening that Job's suffering came from Satan's test of his righteousness, not any sin of his own (1:8-12; 2:3-6). At the close, God rebuked Eliphaz directly: "my wrath is kindled against you and against your two friends, because you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (42:7), and required him to offer a burnt offering while Job prayed for him -- a reversal in which the falsely-accused sufferer intercedes for his accuser.

Eliphaz was one of three friends who came to comfort Job after he lost his family, his health, and his wealth. At first, Eliphaz and the others simply sat with Job in silence for seven days, sharing his grief. But once the conversation began, Eliphaz argued that people only suffer because of sin they've committed, so Job's terrible suffering must mean he had done something to deserve it. He spoke with real conviction, even describing a vision he'd had, but his core argument was too simple and, in the end, wrong. At the end of the book, God directly told Eliphaz and his two friends that they had not spoken correctly about Him, the way His servant Job had, and required them to offer a sacrifice and ask Job to pray for them.

Thought for Today

Through Eliphaz’s experience of retributive theology, God teaches us to trust His wisdom and follow Jesus faithfully.

References: Job 2:11; Job 4:1; Job 15:1; Job 22:1; Job 42:7-9
