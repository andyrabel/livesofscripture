Abraham

Also called: Abram
OT Patriarchal

Called by God out of Mesopotamia with a promise of land, descendants, and blessing to all nations; father of Isaac and, through Hagar, Ishmael; the model in Romans and Hebrews of justification by faith in a promise not yet seen fulfilled.

For younger readers

Abraham trusted God's promise to bless the world through his family, even when it meant leaving everything familiar behind.

Interpretive note: The location of 'Ur of the Chaldeans' (Genesis 11:28) is disputed. It is traditionally identified with the Sumerian city of Ur in southern Mesopotamia, though some scholars argue for a site closer to Haran in the north. Scripture does not resolve the question.

Life Story

Abram was called out of Ur of the Chaldeans, and later Haran, when God commanded him to leave his father's household for a land he had not yet seen, promising to make him into a great nation and to bless all the families of the earth through him (Genesis 12:1-3). He was seventy-five years old, already established, when he obeyed — a pattern of faith Genesis and later Hebrews and Romans hold up as the model of justification: Abram believed God's promise before he ever saw it fulfilled, and it was credited to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6; Romans 4:3; Hebrews 11:8-10). That faith did not make him flawless. Twice he presented his wife Sarai as his sister out of fear for his own life — once to Pharaoh in Egypt, once to Abimelech of Gerar — endangering her to save himself, and both times God had to intervene to protect her and expose Abraham's deception (Genesis 12:10-20; Genesis 20). When the promised son delayed for decades, Abram and Sarai tried to manufacture the fulfillment themselves, and Abram fathered Ishmael through Sarai's servant Hagar, a decision that produced lasting family grief rather than the promise God intended (Genesis 16). God nonetheless kept covenant with Abraham, renaming him from Abram ('exalted father') to Abraham ('father of many') and sealing the promise with the sign of circumcision (Genesis 17). The covenant reached its sharpest test on Mount Moriah, where God commanded Abraham to offer his long-awaited son Isaac as a sacrifice, and Abraham obeyed to the point of raising the knife before the LORD provided a substitute ram — a scene Scripture treats as the clearest Old Testament picture of a father not withholding his son, and one the New Testament reads forward to the greater, non-substituted sacrifice of God's own Son (Genesis 22; Hebrews 11:17-19; Romans 8:32). Abraham died at 175, having seen the promise only in part — a stranger and exile in the land God swore to give his descendants, still looking ahead to a city whose builder and maker is God (Hebrews 11:13-16).

Family

Abraham's household was large and complicated even by ancient standards: his wife Sarah, his concubine Hagar, two sons who could not safely live under one roof, and a nephew, Lot, whom he had raised and protected through war and Sodom's destruction before their households finally separated (Genesis 13; Genesis 14; Genesis 19). Isaac was the son of promise, born to Sarah in her old age after decades of barrenness that had made God's word seem impossible (Genesis 21:1-7). Ishmael, Hagar's son, was sent away with his mother at Sarah's insistence and God's permission, but God did not abandon him — he promised to make a nation of Ishmael too, for Abraham's sake (Genesis 21:8-21). Abraham secured a wife for Isaac from his own kindred rather than the Canaanites, sending his servant back to Mesopotamia to bring back Rebekah, and after Sarah's death he took another wife, Keturah, who bore him several more sons who were sent east, away from Isaac, with gifts, so that Isaac alone would inherit the covenant promise (Genesis 24; Genesis 25:1-6). Every branch of Abraham's family — Isaac's line to Israel, Ishmael's line, Keturah's sons — becomes ethnically significant later in the biblical story, but the covenant line runs through Isaac alone, by God's choice rather than birth order or merit (Romans 9:7-9).
