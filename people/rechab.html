Rechab (M)

First named in 2 Samuel 4:2-9

OT United Monarchy

Rechab, a Benjamite, served as one of the army captains under Ish-bosheth, Saul's son who reigned as a rival king over Israel while David ruled Judah (2 Samuel 4:2). After Abner's death left Ish-bosheth weak and undefended, Rechab and his brother Baanah entered the king's house at midday while he lay resting and killed him, beheading him before fleeing through the night toward Hebron (4:5-7). They carried the severed head to David, presenting the murder as an act of loyalty and vengeance on David's behalf, expecting a reward (4:8). David rejected the framing entirely, recalling how he had previously executed the man who merely claimed to have killed Saul, and asked how much more he owed justice to the killers of "a righteous man in his own house on his bed" (4:9-11). David had Rechab and Baanah executed, their hands and feet cut off, and their bodies hung publicly by the pool in Hebron, while Ish-bosheth's head was buried with honor in Abner's tomb (4:12). Rechab's story shows that political murder, even when aimed at a rival's downfall, was never something David would reward -- it was simply cold-blooded murder.

Rechab was a captain in the army of Ish-bosheth, one of King Saul's surviving sons who had briefly reigned over Israel while David reigned only over Judah. Rechab and his brother Baanah decided to assassinate Ish-bosheth while he was resting at midday, thinking this would please David and earn them a reward, since David and Ish-bosheth's side were rivals. They carried Ish-bosheth's head all the way to David expecting to be praised. Instead, David was horrified, condemned them for murdering an innocent, defenseless man, and had them executed. Rechab's story is a warning about pursuing ambition through violence and assuming wrongly what will please those in power.

Thought for Today

Rechab murdered a defenseless, sleeping king hoping for David's reward, but David called it what it was: sin. Jesus, our King, never rewards evil done in His name.
